# CrisisRoute Evaluation Results

Generated by [`run_eval.py`](run_eval.py) across 30 seeds (`seed = 0..29`) at
two scales — **small** (the default ~80-node generator, 4-8 zones, 5-14
resources, varies per seed) and **large** (the new `large` preset: ~300
nodes, 15 zones, 25 resources, fixed across seeds) — plus `flood_demo` for
the single-run convergence curve. Raw data in [results/](results/), charts
in [figures/](figures/). All numbers below are copied directly from the
script's own printed output — nothing here is estimated or rounded beyond
what the script printed.

## 1. Routing: BFS vs DFS vs A*

30 seeds × 10 random start/goal pairs per seed = 300 searches per algorithm,
at small scale. See [routing_comparison.csv](results/routing_comparison.csv)
and [routing_comparison.png](figures/routing_comparison.png).

| algorithm | cost mean (h) | cost std | nodes mean | nodes std | time mean (ms) | time std |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| astar | 0.4947 | 0.2221 | 24.32 | 17.38 | 0.0962 | 0.0680 |
| bfs   | 0.6306 | 0.3347 | 42.75 | 23.03 | 0.0386 | 0.0203 |
| dfs   | 2.2047 | 1.2979 | 41.27 | 22.01 | 0.0486 | 0.0383 |

A* finds the cheapest path on average (0.49h vs 0.63h for BFS and 2.20h for
DFS) while also exploring the fewest nodes of the three (24.3 vs 42.8 for BFS
and 41.3 for DFS) — the heuristic is doing real work, not just adding
overhead. DFS's path cost has by far the highest mean and the widest spread
(std 1.30h, roughly half its own mean), consistent with it returning
whatever path it happens to find first rather than a short one. A* is the
slowest in wall-clock time (0.096ms vs ~0.04-0.05ms for BFS/DFS) because it
maintains a priority queue and evaluates a heuristic per node, which
BFS/DFS don't — a real but small cost given how much shorter and cheaper
the paths it returns are. (Path cost and nodes-explored are identical to
every previous run of this script — only the wall-clock timing columns
shift between runs, since they depend on momentary machine load rather than
the algorithms themselves.)

## 2. Allocation: GA vs Greedy, small vs large scale

30 seeds, one GA run (default `GAConfig`) and one greedy run per seed, at
both scales, against the same risk assessments and cost matrix. See
[allocation_comparison.csv](results/allocation_comparison.csv) and
[allocation_comparison.png](figures/allocation_comparison.png).

**Small scale** (4-8 zones, 5-14 resources, random per seed):

| algorithm | pop. covered mean | std | avg response (h) mean | std | utilisation mean | std | travel cost (h) mean | std |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| GA     | 33254.1 | 13433.4 | 0.3370 | 0.1402 | 0.770 | 0.235 | 2.249 | 1.042 |
| Greedy | 24745.9 |  9971.1 | 0.3852 | 0.1496 | 0.703 | 0.263 | 2.378 | 1.099 |

GA fitness ≥ greedy fitness in **30/30 seeds**.

**Large scale** (`large` preset: 15 zones, 25 resources, fixed):

| algorithm | pop. covered mean | std | avg response (h) mean | std | utilisation mean | std | travel cost (h) mean | std |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| GA     | 85299.5 | 17470.5 | 0.6311 | 0.1376 | 0.788 | 0.132 | 12.326 | 3.057 |
| Greedy | 74083.4 | 11333.9 | 0.6960 | 0.1469 | 0.761 | 0.135 | 13.324 | 4.000 |

GA fitness ≥ greedy fitness in **21/30 seeds**.

GA beats greedy on population covered at both scales (+34% at small scale,
+15% at large scale), and on every other metric too at both scales (lower
average response time, higher utilisation, lower travel cost — e.g. travel
cost is 7.5% lower for GA at large scale, response time 9.3% lower). But
**GA's edge over greedy is narrower and less consistent at large scale**:
the population-covered advantage drops from 34% to 15%, and GA no longer
wins on fitness in every seed (21/30, down from 30/30). This is the
expected effect of a fixed GA budget (`GAConfig` defaults: population 80,
generations 150) against a search space that grows exponentially with
problem size — see the search space sizes below — while greedy's
zone-by-zone construction scales roughly linearly and doesn't lose ground
the same way.

### Search space size

`(num_zones + 1) ^ num_resources` — the number of possible chromosomes
(every resource independently idle or assigned to one of the zones).

```
small  zones=[4, 5, 6, 7, 8] resources=[6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14]
       min=1.562e+04  max=9.689e+10  mean=1.293e+10
large  zones=[15] resources=[25]
       min=1.268e+30  max=1.268e+30  mean=1.268e+30
flood_demo (4 zones, 5 resources)      = 3,125
large preset (15 zones, 25 resources)  = 1.268e+30  (1,267,650,600,228,229,401,496,703,205,376 exactly)
```

The large preset's search space is about **4.06 × 10²⁶ times bigger** than
flood_demo's — the GA population/generation budget did not grow at all
between the two (same default `GAConfig` for both), which is exactly why
section 3 below shows convergence taking substantially longer at large
scale, and why GA's win margin over greedy narrows there.

### Convergence-speed check requested for the large preset

The question to check was: *if the GA still converges in under 10
generations on the large preset, investigate why before changing anything.*
It does not — see [GA convergence by scale](#4-ga-convergence-by-scale)
below for the actual numbers (mean plateau generation 94.3, minimum across
all 30 seeds 50). No investigation was needed and nothing was changed.

## 3. Replanning: static vs dynamic, plus replanning cost

30 seeds × 4 scenario types (`no_change`, `road_blockage`, `new_zone`,
`resource_shortage`), small scale. "static" evaluates the original plan's
fixed routes under the new conditions (a blocked route or an unavailable
resource counts as a failed delivery); "dynamic" is `Planner.apply_event`'s
replanned result. See
[replanning_comparison.csv](results/replanning_comparison.csv),
[replanning_comparison.png](figures/replanning_comparison.png) and
[replanning_cost.png](figures/replanning_cost.png).

| scenario_type | mode | pop. covered mean | std | failed deliveries mean | std | avg response (h) mean | std |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| no_change | static  | 33254.1 | 13433.4 | 0.00 | 0.00 | 0.3370 | 0.1402 |
| no_change | dynamic | 33254.1 | 13433.4 | 0.00 | 0.00 | 0.3370 | 0.1402 |
| road_blockage | static  | 20249.4 | 11180.7 | 2.93 | 1.46 | 0.3148 | 0.1663 |
| road_blockage | dynamic | 32761.0 | 13890.0 | 0.00 | 0.00 | 0.3895 | 0.1512 |
| new_zone | static  | 33254.1 | 13433.4 | 0.00 | 0.00 | 0.3370 | 0.1402 |
| new_zone | dynamic | 39633.6 | 11520.8 | 0.00 | 0.00 | 0.3669 | 0.1195 |
| resource_shortage | static  | 30115.3 | 12197.8 | 0.87 | 0.35 | 0.3329 | 0.1395 |
| resource_shortage | dynamic | 31098.8 | 12531.4 | 0.00 | 0.00 | 0.3324 | 0.1404 |

`no_change` is a sanity check — with no event applied, static and dynamic
are by construction identical, and the numbers confirm that exactly.

`road_blockage` is where dynamic replanning matters most: the static plan
loses 2.93 deliveries on average and its covered population drops from the
33254 baseline to 20249 — a 39% loss. Replanning recovers almost all of it
(32761, within 2% of the undisturbed baseline) and eliminates every failed
delivery, at the cost of a higher average response time (0.390h vs the
static plan's 0.315h — the static figure is measured only over the
deliveries that didn't fail, which understates its own cost).

`new_zone` shows the static baseline flatlined at the pre-event number
(33254.1, identical to `no_change`) because a plan built before the zone
existed has literally no route to it — the zone is 100% uncovered under the
static policy. Replanning raises covered population to 39634.

`resource_shortage` costs the static plan 0.87 failed deliveries on average
and about 1000 in covered population (30115 vs 31099 dynamic) — smaller
than the other two because only one resource is affected.

Across all three real event types, **dynamic replanning drove failed
deliveries to exactly 0.00**. The one place static comes out ahead is
average response time on `road_blockage` and `resource_shortage` — a
measurement artifact: `average_response_time_hours` is only averaged over
deliveries that actually complete, so a plan with fewer (but failed)
deliveries has an easier average to hit.

### Replanning cost

New this round: how expensive is a replan, and what kind did it do —
reroute only (cost stayed within 30% and stayed reachable, allocation
unchanged) or a full GA rerun?

| scenario_type | replan time (ms) mean | std | assignments changed mean | std | routes changed mean | std | full_rerun count | reroute_only count |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| no_change | 0.000 | 0.000 | 0.00 | 0.00 | 0.00 | 0.00 | 0 | 0 |
| road_blockage | 85.187 | 93.823 | 1.47 | 2.46 | 3.70 | 2.23 | 14 | 16 |
| new_zone | 200.808 | 26.843 | 3.50 | 2.30 | 3.50 | 2.30 | 30 | 0 |
| resource_shortage | 173.490 | 26.942 | 3.37 | 2.48 | 3.37 | 2.48 | 30 | 0 |

`new_zone` and `resource_shortage` are a full GA rerun on every single one
of the 30 seeds — by design, both event handlers always call `run_ga`
(seeded with the current allocation, for `new_zone`). `road_blockage` is
the only event type that can go either way, and it actually does: **14 of
30 seeds triggered a full GA rerun, 16 of 30 only needed a reroute.**
Splitting `road_blockage`'s replan time by which path it took makes the
point starkly — reroute-only replans average **0.375ms** (std 0.202,
n=16), full GA reruns average **182.1ms** (std 25.8, n=14): a full rerun
costs about **486× longer** than a reroute. This is exactly what the
"reroute only unless cost rises more than 30% or becomes unreachable"
design in `Planner._apply_road_blocked` is for — most road blockages don't
need the expensive path.

## 4. GA convergence by scale

Reuses the 30×2 GA runs from section 2 (same seeds, same default
`GAConfig`) to look at how many generations each scale needs to reach its
own best-ever fitness, and how much diversity (gap between best and average
fitness) remains once it gets there. See
[ga_convergence_by_scale.csv](results/ga_convergence_by_scale.csv) and
[ga_convergence_by_scale.png](figures/ga_convergence_by_scale.png).

```
small  best-fitness-plateau generation: mean=21.3 std=23.3 min=3 max=80
       final best-minus-average fitness gap: mean=2281.9 std=936.3
large  best-fitness-plateau generation: mean=94.3 std=32.8 min=50 max=145
       final best-minus-average fitness gap: mean=13829.7 std=2497.8
```

**The large preset does not converge in under 10 generations** — its
fastest-converging seed out of 30 still took 50 generations, and the mean
is 94.3, more than 4× the small-scale mean of 21.3. That tracks with the
~4×10²⁶-fold larger search space computed in section 2: a bigger landscape
genuinely takes the same fixed population/mutation budget longer to climb.
The best-minus-average fitness gap is about 6.1× wider at large scale
(13830 vs 2282, mean of final generation). That's proportionally more than
the gap between the two scales' final best-fitness magnitudes themselves
(mean final best fitness -50253 at large scale vs -22090 at small scale, a
2.3× difference) — so the spread doesn't just track the fitness scale, it
grows faster than that. A plausible reading: 25 resources each mutate
independently (random-reset, rate 0.05) every generation, so the large-scale
population has roughly 5× as many independent noise sources per individual
as the small-scale one (5-14 resources), and more independent noise sources
compounds into more fitness variance across the population, not just a
bigger absolute scale. That's a hypothesis from this run's numbers, not a
separately verified claim. What the numbers do establish directly either
way: both scales keep a wide, non-collapsing best/average gap all the way
to generation 150, which is the actual question that matters here — neither
scale shows the population collapsing onto one genotype. No pathology
found, so per the brief, nothing was changed.

## 5. GA convergence on flood_demo

Single run, `GAConfig(seed=42)`, default population/generation settings —
kept as the one illustrative worked example referenced elsewhere in the
project (separate from the 30-seed aggregate in section 4). See
[ga_convergence.csv](results/ga_convergence.csv) and
[ga_convergence.png](figures/ga_convergence.png).

- Generations run: 150
- Generation 0 best / average fitness: -79131.6 / -94212.5
- **Best fitness first reached at generation 4: -78635.0**
- Final generation average fitness: -79584.5

The run converges almost immediately — the best-ever fitness line is flat
from generation 4 onward (elitism means it can only hold or improve), while
the average-fitness line (from generation 10 onward, past the initial
climb) oscillates between about 496 and 2056 below the best and never
catches up. That's random-reset mutation and tournament selection
continuing to inject fresh, usually-worse individuals every generation even
after the population has found a strong solution — the population keeps
exploring instead of collapsing onto a single genotype. This single-seed
flood_demo result (plateau at generation 4) is on the fast end even for
small scale (section 4's small-scale mean across 30 seeds is generation
21.3) — seed 42 happens to be a quick-converging one, which is exactly why
section 4's 30-seed aggregate, not a single seed, is the number to trust
for a general claim about convergence speed.
